# Rewritten Exposé XI, section XI.1 — projective spaces, unirational varieties, rationally connected varieties

## What this file is about

A line, a plane, a three-dimensional space — and then the higher-dimensional analogues. A projective space is the
natural geometric home for these, the place where parallel lines do meet at infinity and where every algebraic equation
finds enough room to behave. The simplest projective spaces have no loops to wind around; nothing in them is twisted
enough to support a non-trivial cover. The same holds for any geometric object whose points can all be reached by
sliding along curves of the simplest possible shape — straight lines or their generalisations. Such objects sit at the
gentle end of a landscape where most algebraic shapes have rich and tangled topology, and isolating them sets the
baseline for everything that follows.

Files 48–51 closed Exposé X with a structural arc: how $\pi_{1}$ of a geometric fibre behaves along a specialisation
`y₀ ⤳ y₁` in a proper separable family $f : X \to Y$. The arc had three peaks. **Upper semicontinuity** (X.2.3, file
49): the specialisation map $sp : \pi_{1}(\bar{X}_{y_{1}}) \to \pi_{1}(\bar{X}_{y_{0}})$ is surjective. **The
transcendental computation** (X.2.6, file 50): for a proper smooth connected curve of genus $g$, $\pi_{1}$ is
topologically generated by `2g` elements bound by the surface relation. **Continuity** (X.3.8, file 51): the
specialisation map is an *isomorphism* on prime-to-$p$ quotients when $f$ is proper smooth.

Exposé XI is structurally different. It is not a single theorem-driven arc. It is a collection of **examples and
complements**: specific schemes (projective spaces, abelian varieties, projective cones, the Fermat surface) whose
$\pi_{1}$ Grothendieck computes by feeding them to the Exposé X machinery, alongside specific cohomological tools (the
Kummer and Artin–Schreier exact sequences) that pin down $\pi^{ab}_{1}$ of proper schemes through their relation to the
Picard group. We split Exposé XI across five gentle files: this one (§XI.1, projective spaces and rational varieties),
file 53 (§XI.2, abelian varieties and the Tate module), file 54 (§XI.3, projective cones and Zariski's flat
counterexample), file 55 (§§XI.4–XI.5, principal bundles in the descent-theoretic sense), and file 56 (§XI.6, Kummer and
Artin–Schreier theories).

This file (§XI.1) opens the example arc with the simplest non-trivial case: **projective space $\mathbb{P}^{r}$ is
simply connected** (XI.1.1). The proof has two routes through the Exposé X machinery — one via the inductive
Lefschetz-style surjection X.2.10, the other via the product formula X.1.7 and birational invariance X.3.4. Both routes
generalise: **every rational normal proper variety is simply connected** (XI.1.2), and **every unirational normal
variety has finite $\pi_{1}$** (XI.1.3). The gentle closing §XI.1.4 then opens out into modern context: Serre's
transcendental theorem that smooth projective unirational varieties in characteristic zero are simply connected, the
distinction between *weakly* and *strongly* unirational in characteristic $p > 0$ and Shioda's supersingular
counterexample, the Artin–Mumford and Murre examples of unirational non-rational threefolds, and the modern definition
of **rationally connected** varieties together with Kollár's theorem that separably rationally connected varieties have
trivial étale fundamental group and the Graber–Harris–Starr theorem on RC families.

The slogan that emerges by the end of this file: **a smooth projective variety with "enough rational curves" has small
$\pi_{1}$** — and the natural notion of "enough rational curves" is *separable rational connectedness*. In
characteristic zero, rational connectedness suffices; in characteristic $p > 0$, the separability hypothesis is real.

## §XI.1.1 — Projective space is simply connected

<!-- source: XI.1.1 -->

### Theorem XI.1.1 — $\pi_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{r}) = 0$

<!-- source: XI.1.1 -->

**Theorem XI.1.1.** Let $k$ be an algebraically closed field and let $X = \mathbb{P}^{r}_{k}$ be projective space of
dimension $r$ over $k$. Then $X$ is **simply connected**, that is, $\pi_{1}(X) = 0$.

*This result says:* every connected finite étale covering of $\mathbb{P}^{r}_{k}$ is trivial — isomorphic to
$\mathbb{P}^{r}_{k}$ itself. There are no non-trivial "covers" of projective space in the algebraic-geometric sense,
just as there are none in the topological sense over $\mathbb{C}$.

We give **two proofs**, both of which illuminate machinery from Exposé X. The first proof handles $r = 1$ by the
Riemann–Hurwitz formula and the inductive step by the hyperplane-section surjectivity X.2.10 (file 50). The second proof
reduces $\mathbb{P}^{r}$ to a product of $\mathbb{P}^{1}$-factors via birational invariance X.3.4 (file 51).

#### Proof 1 (genus formula plus hyperplane induction).

*This is the proof Grothendieck gives in the SGA source.*

**Step 1 ($r = 0$).** The space $\mathbb{P}^{0}_{k} = \operatorname{Spec} k$ is a single point, and
$\pi_{1}(\operatorname{Spec} k) = \operatorname{Gal}(\bar{k} / k) = 0$ since $k$ is algebraically closed. So
$\pi_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{0}) = 0$.

**Step 2 ($r = 1$).** We show that any connected finite étale covering $X' \to X = \mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}$ is trivial. The
base $X = \mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}$ is a smooth projective curve of genus $g = 0$. Since $X' \to X$ is étale, the cover $X'$
is a smooth projective curve with no ramification. Let $g'$ be its genus and $d = [X' : X]$ the degree. The
**Riemann–Hurwitz formula** for an unramified cover of smooth projective curves (see appendix
[`riemann-hurwitz-curves`](appendix-black-boxes.md#riemann-hurwitz-curves)) says

$$
2g' - 2 = d \cdot (2g - 2),
$$

equivalently $1 - g' = d \cdot (1 - g)$. Plugging $g = 0$ gives $1 - g' = d$. Since $g' \geq 0$ always, this forces
$d \leq 1$, hence $d = 1$, and so $X' \simeq X = \mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}$. Connected étale covers of $\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}$ are
trivial; $\pi_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}) = 0$.

**Step 3 ($r \geq 2$).** We induct on $r$. Suppose $\pi_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{r-1}_{k}) = 0$. Apply X.2.10 (file 50,
hyperplane surjectivity) to $\mathbb{P}^{r}_{k}$ and a hyperplane $H = \mathbb{P}^{r-1}_{k} \subset \mathbb{P}^{r}_{k}$:
the inclusion induces a **surjection**

$$
\pi_{1}(H) \twoheadrightarrow \pi_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{r}_{k}).
$$

By the inductive hypothesis $\pi_{1}(H) = 0$. A surjection from the trivial group has trivial codomain, so
$\pi_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{r}_{k}) = 0$. ∎

#### Proof 2 (product formula plus birational invariance).

*This is the alternative proof Grothendieck mentions at XI.1.1.*

**Step 1.** The product
$(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k})^{r} = \mathbb{P}^{1}_{k} \times \mathbb{P}^{1}_{k} \times \cdots \times \mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}$ is
simply connected. Indeed, by the **product formula** X.1.7 (file 48), for $X$ and $Y$ proper connected over an
algebraically closed field $k$, we have $\pi_{1}(X \times_{k} Y) \simeq \pi_{1}(X) \times \pi_{1}(Y)$. Iterating this
$r$ times,

$$
\pi_{1}((\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k})^{r}) \simeq \pi_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k})^{r} = 0,
$$

using $\pi_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k}) = 0$ from Step 2 of Proof 1.

**Step 2.** The product $(\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k})^{r}$ is **birational** to $\mathbb{P}^{r}_{k}$ — both are smooth proper
rational varieties of dimension $r$. By **birational invariance** of the étale fundamental group, X.3.4 (file 51),
$\pi_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{r}_{k}) \simeq \pi_{1}((\mathbb{P}^{1}_{k})^{r}) = 0$. ∎

> **Slogan.** *$\pi_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{r}) = 0$.* Two proofs: the Lefschetz route (hyperplane induction) routes through
> X.2.10; the birational route ($\mathbb{P}^{r}$ ~ $(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{r}$) routes through X.1.7 and X.3.4.

#### Aside (why both proofs matter).

The two proofs of Theorem XI.1.1 are not just different routes to the same conclusion. Proof 1 generalises directly to
**hyperplane sections of any simply connected smooth variety**: if $X$ is simply connected and $Y \subset X$ is a smooth
hyperplane section in dimension $\geq 2$, then $Y$ is simply connected. This is the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem for
$\pi_{1}$, foreshadowed at X.2.13 (file 50) and proved in full in SGA 2 X.3.10 (appendix
[`sga2-lefschetz-pi1`](appendix-black-boxes.md#sga2-lefschetz-pi1)).

Proof 2 generalises in a different direction. It shows that **any smooth proper variety birational to a product of
simply connected smooth proper varieties is simply connected.** This is the gateway to the general result XI.1.2 below.

## §XI.1.2 — Rational normal proper ⇒ simply connected

<!-- source: XI.1.2 -->

### Corollary XI.1.2 — rational ⇒ simply connected

<!-- source: XI.1.2 -->

A **rational variety** over a field $k$ is a proper integral $k$-scheme whose function field $k(X)$ is a purely
transcendental extension of $k$ — that is, $k(X) \simeq k(t_{1}, \cdots, t_{r})$ for some $r = \dim X$. Geometrically,
this means $X$ is birational to $\mathbb{P}^{r}_{k}$.

**Corollary XI.1.2.** Let $X$ be a normal proper integral $k$-scheme over an algebraically closed field $k$. If $X$ is a
rational variety, then $X$ is simply connected: $\pi_{1}(X) = 0$.

*This result says:* normality + properness + rationality together force trivial étale fundamental group. The role of
normality is to make X.3.4 (file 51) applicable — birational invariance of $\pi_{1}$ requires the relevant schemes to be
smooth or at least regular along the locus of comparison, and for a proper normal scheme this is automatic on a dense
open.

**Proof.** Since $X$ is rational and proper normal, $X$ is birational to $\mathbb{P}^{r}_{k}$ over $k$. By birational
invariance X.3.4 (file 51), $\pi_{1}(X) \simeq \pi_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{r}_{k})$. By XI.1.1, the right side is `0`. ∎

> **Slogan.** *Rational normal proper ⇒ simply connected.* Birational invariance reduces the problem to projective
> space.

#### Application — Grassmann varieties.

For each pair $(k, n)$ with $0 \leq k \leq n$, the Grassmann variety $Gr(k, n)_{k}$ parametrising $k$-dimensional
subspaces of $k^{n}$ is a smooth projective $k$-variety. It is rational: in the standard Plücker chart, choose $k$ rows
of an $n \times k$ matrix to be the identity matrix, and the remaining $(n-k)\cdot k$ entries parametrise an open dense
affine subset of $Gr(k, n)$. So $Gr(k, n)$ is birational to $\mathbb{A}^{k(n-k)}_{k}$, hence to
$\mathbb{P}^{k(n-k)}_{k}$. By XI.1.2 (with $k$ algebraically closed), **$\pi_{1}(Gr(k, n)) = 0$**.

#### Application — $G/P$ for $G$ linear and $P$ parabolic.

More generally, let $G$ be a connected linear algebraic group over an algebraically closed field $k$, and let
$P \subset G$ be a **parabolic subgroup** — a closed subgroup such that $G/P$ is a projective variety, equivalently a
subgroup containing a Borel subgroup. Then $G/P$ is a rational variety. The proof uses the **Bruhat decomposition** of
$G$: the double-coset space `B \ G / B` is in bijection with the Weyl group $W = N_{G}(T) / T$ of $G$, and the unique
open Bruhat cell $Bw_{0}B / B$ (where $w_{0}$ is the longest element) is a dense open affine in $G/B$. Composing with
the projection $G/B \to G/P$ gives an open dense affine in $G/P$, hence $G/P$ is rational. By XI.1.2,
**$\pi_{1}(G/P) = 0$** for any parabolic $P \subset G$ in a connected linear algebraic group.

(The Bruhat decomposition is the standard tool in Lie theory and algebraic groups; see Borel *Linear Algebraic Groups*,
GTM 126, §11.4, or Springer *Linear Algebraic Groups*, Prog. Math. 9, §8.5. Appendix
[`borel-springer-bruhat`](appendix-black-boxes.md#borel-springer-bruhat).)

In particular, **flag varieties** $G/B$ (with $B$ a Borel) and all generalised flag varieties $G/P$ are simply
connected. This recovers and generalises the Grassmann case.

## §XI.1.3 — Unirational normal ⇒ finite π₁

<!-- source: XI.1.3 -->

### Definition XI.1.3 — unirational variety

<!-- source: XI.1.3 -->

**Definition.** A **unirational variety over $k$** is a proper integral $k$-scheme $X$ whose function field $k(X)$ is
contained in a purely transcendental extension $K' = k(t_{1}, \cdots, t_{r})$ of $k$ of the same transcendence degree
$r = \dim X$. Equivalently, there exists a **dominant rational map**

$$
f : \mathbb{P}^{r}_{k} \dashrightarrow X
$$

with $r = \dim X$.

The vocabulary contrasts with **rational** (XI.1.2): a rational variety has *function field equal to* a purely
transcendental extension; a unirational variety has function field *contained in* one. So every rational variety is
unirational, but in general the converse fails — this is the *unirationality problem* of classical algebraic geometry,
to which we return in §XI.1.4 below.

#### Aside (weak vs strong unirational).

The above is what M. Raynaud (2003 addendum to the SGA source) calls **weakly unirational**. There is a finer notion in
characteristic $p > 0$:

- **Weakly unirational over $k$**: function field $k(X) \subset k(t_{1}, \cdots, t_{r})$, with no separability
  hypothesis on the embedding.
- **Strongly unirational over $k$**: additionally, $k(X) \subset k(t_{1}, \cdots, t_{r})$ is a **separable** field
  extension — equivalently, the rational map $f : \mathbb{P}^{r} \dashrightarrow X$ is *generically smooth*.

In **characteristic zero**, every finite field extension is separable, so weak = strong: there is just "unirational." In
**characteristic $p > 0$** the two notions can differ, and the distinction is load-bearing for the unirationality
question (Shioda's example, §XI.1.4 below). For Definition XI.1.3 and Corollary XI.1.4 below, "unirational" means weakly
unirational (the SGA's default).

### Corollary XI.1.4 — unirational normal ⇒ finite π₁

<!-- source: XI.1.3 -->

**Corollary XI.1.4.** *(Labelled `XI.1.3` in the SGA source.)* Let $X$ be a normal integral proper $k$-scheme over an
algebraically closed field $k$. If $X$ is unirational, then $\pi_{1}(X)$ is **finite**.

*This result says:* unirationality alone (without rationality) bounds $\pi_{1}$ to be finite, but does not in general
force it to be trivial. The bound depends on the unirationality degree.

**Proof.** Let $K = k(X)$ and let $K' = k(t_{1}, \cdots, t_{r})$ be a purely transcendental extension of $k$ containing
$K$, with $[K' : K] < \infty$ (note: as stated in the definition, the unirationality definition gives
$r = tr.deg_{k} K = tr.deg_{k} K'$; the requirement that $K' / K$ be a *finite* extension is implicit in some statements
but follows from the dominant rational map having generic finite fibre).

Let $X' \to X$ be a connected finite étale covering, with function field $L / K$ finite separable. We must show that $L$
is bounded (independent of the choice of cover) by a function of $K' / K$.

**Step 1.** Consider the tensor product $L \otimes_{K} K'$. The corresponding morphism on the model side is the rational
map

```text
ℙ^r_k ⤏ X ⤌ X′ \quad\text{giving rise to}\quad \widetilde{X′} = X′ ×_X ℙ^r_k → ℙ^r_k.
```

The "reflections preceding X.3.4" (file 51, §X.3.4) — birational invariance of $\pi_{1}$ and the étale-extension
property of normal models — tell us that this rational map can be resolved into a genuine étale
$\mathbb{P}^{r}_{k}$-covering: the normalisation of $\mathbb{P}^{r}_{k}$ in $L \otimes_{K} K'$ is **unramified** over
$\mathbb{P}^{r}_{k}$ because $X' \to X$ is unramified and $\mathbb{P}^{r}_{k} \dashrightarrow X$ is dominant. Hence
$L \otimes_{K} K'$ corresponds to a finite étale $\mathbb{P}^{r}_{k}$-covering.

**Step 2.** By XI.1.1, the only such covering is the trivial one: $L \otimes_{K} K' \simeq K'^{[L:K]}$ as $K'$-algebras
(completely decomposed). This means $L$ embeds as a sub-extension of some product copy of $K' / K$:

$$
L \hookrightarrow K'.
$$

**Step 3.** Hence every finite separable extension $L / K$ corresponding to a connected étale covering $X' \to X$ is
contained in $K' / K$. By the standard fact (file 33 V.8.2; the fundamental group of $\operatorname{Spec} k$ of a field
is the absolute Galois group), $\pi_{1}(X)$ is the inverse limit of $\operatorname{Gal}(L / K)$ as $L$ ranges over
finite Galois extensions of $K$ inside a fixed separable closure. The previous step bounds these $L$ inside $K'$, which
has only finitely many intermediate fields (by Lüroth's theorem in dimension 1, and more generally by the Galois theory
of $K' / K$ once we go to the Galois closure of $K' / K$).

More precisely: let $M / K$ be the Galois closure of $K' / K$ inside a separable closure $K^{sep}$. Then every $L /K$ in
the previous step is contained in $M$, and $\pi_{1}(X)$ quotients onto $\operatorname{Gal}(M / K)$, which is finite. ∎

> **Slogan.** *Unirational normal ⇒ $\pi_{1}$ finite.* Bounded by the Galois closure of $K' / K$.

## §XI.1.4 — Modern context: Serre, Shioda, Artin–Mumford, rationally connected varieties

1. Serre's transcendental theorem (1959) that smooth projective unirational varieties over an algebraically closed field

1. The distinction between *weakly* and *strongly* unirational in characteristic $p > 0$, with Shioda's counterexample

We treat each in turn as a separate gentle subsection. The mathematics is all post-SGA-1; the references are modern

### XI.1.4.1 — Serre's transcendental theorem (1959)

<!-- source: XI.1.4 -->

**Theorem (Serre, 1959).** Let $X$ be a smooth projective unirational variety over an algebraically closed field $k$ of
**characteristic zero**. Then $\pi_{1}(X) = 0$.

*This result says:* in characteristic zero, the conclusion of XI.1.4 can be strengthened from "finite" to "trivial." The
proof is **transcendental** — it uses Hodge theory, not algebraic methods.

**Idea of proof.** Embed $k \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}$ (possible since $char k = 0$ and $k$ is algebraically closed,
hence contains a copy of $\bar{\mathbb{Q}}$, and any embedding $\bar{\mathbb{Q}} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}$ extends by
transcendence-basis arguments). Base-changing, we may assume $k = \mathbb{C}$. Let $X^{an}$ be the associated
complex-analytic space. By the **GAGA comparison** (Exposé XII of SGA 1, not yet gentle),
$\pi_{1}(X) = \pi_{1}(X^{an})^{\widehat{\,}}$ — the profinite completion of the topological fundamental group of
$X^{an}$.

To show $\pi_{1}(X^{an}) = 0$, use **Hodge symmetry**: for a smooth projective complex variety,
$h^{p, q}(X) = h^{q, p}(X)$, where $h^{p, q} = \dim H^{q}(X, \Omega^{p}_{X})$. In particular
$h^{1, 0}(X) = h^{0, 1}(X)$. For a unirational $X$ in characteristic zero, the existence of a dominant rational
$f : \mathbb{P}^{r} \dashrightarrow X$ gives a sequence of compatible blow-ups resolving $f$ to an honest morphism, and
the resulting maps on cohomology force $h^{1, 0}(X) = \dim H^{0}(X, \Omega^{1}_{X}) = 0$ (unirational varieties in
characteristic zero have no nonzero holomorphic 1-forms). By Hodge symmetry $h^{0, 1}(X) = 0$, so
$H^{1}(X^{an}, \mathbb{Z}) = 0$ topologically. Since $X^{an}$ is simply connected iff $H^{1}(X^{an}, \mathbb{Z}) = 0$
*and* the higher-fundamental-group obstruction vanishes — but for the **simply connected** statement we actually need
slightly more: Hodge symmetry combined with Lefschetz–Hopf-style arguments gives that *all* of $\pi_{1}(X^{an})$
vanishes (Serre's argument fills in this last step, citing the topology of complex hypersurfaces).

(Modern reference: Serre's original paper, J. London Math. Soc. 34 (1959), 481–484, appendix
[`serre-uniration-pi1`](appendix-black-boxes.md#serre-uniration-pi1); for the GAGA comparison, see Exposé XII of SGA 1
or Hartshorne, *Algebraic Geometry*, Appendix B.)

> **Slogan.** *In characteristic zero, smooth projective unirational ⇒ simply connected.* Hodge symmetry forces
> $h^{1, 0} = 0$, hence $H^{1} = 0$, hence $\pi^{ab}_{1} = 0$; for unirational varieties the higher obstructions also
> vanish.

### XI.1.4.2 — Shioda's example: weakly unirational ≠ rational in char $p > 0$

<!-- source: XI.1.4 -->

Serre's transcendental theorem fails in characteristic $p > 0$. T. Shioda constructed (1977) smooth projective **weakly
unirational surfaces** $X / \bar{k}_{p}$ with **non-trivial** finite fundamental group (finite by XI.1.4 above), and
hence *non-rational* (since rational ⇒ $\pi_{1} = 0$ by XI.1.2).

**Theorem (Shioda 1977).** For every prime $p \geq 5$ there exist smooth projective surfaces $X / \bar{\mathbb{F}}_{p}$
that are weakly unirational but have $\pi_{1}(X)$ non-trivial, hence are non-rational.

Shioda's construction uses **supersingular K3 surfaces** — K3 surfaces all of whose Picard cycles are algebraic. These
have purely inseparable covers by superspecial abelian surfaces, witnessing weak unirationality, while their finite
fundamental groups are computed from the Brauer group and torsion in $\operatorname{Pic}^{0}$. The full details require
characteristic-$p$-specific tools (Picard scheme jumps, slope filtration).

The contrast with characteristic zero:

| Setting                              | Smooth projective unirational $\Rightarrow$                                           |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| $char k = 0$                         | simply connected (Serre)                                                              |
| $char k = p > 0$, **strongly** unir. | rational (Castelnuovo, surface case; Kollár, higher-dim under sRC) ⇒ simply connected |
| $char k = p > 0$, **weakly** unir.   | finite $\pi_{1}$ (XI.1.4); may be non-rational (Shioda)                               |

So in characteristic $p > 0$, the separability of the unirationality witness is what bridges the gap to rationality.

By contrast, **Castelnuovo's rationality criterion** (a smooth projective surface $X / \bar{k}$ is rational iff
$p_{g} = q = 0$ and the second plurigenus vanishes; extended by Zariski to char $p > 0$) tells us that **strongly
unirational surfaces are rational**, hence simply connected. This is consistent with the Shioda example: Shioda's
surfaces are only *weakly* unirational, not strongly.

(References: T. Shioda, "On unirationality of supersingular surfaces," Math. Ann. 225 (1977), 155–159, appendix
[`shioda-supersingular-unirational`](appendix-black-boxes.md#shioda-supersingular-unirational); Castelnuovo–Zariski
rationality criterion, J.-P. Serre, Séminaire Bourbaki no. 146, 1957, *Œuvres* vol. 1, p. 467.)

### XI.1.4.3 — Artin–Mumford and Murre: unirational ≠ rational in dimension $\geq 3$

<!-- source: XI.1.4 -->

The next layer of the unirationality story: in characteristic zero (where Serre's theorem makes $\pi_{1}$ not the
distinguishing invariant), there nevertheless exist smooth projective **unirational non-rational** threefolds.

**Theorem (Clemens–Griffiths, Iskovskikh–Manin, Artin–Mumford, 1971–72).** A smooth cubic hypersurface
$X \subset \mathbb{P}^{4}_{\mathbb{C}}$ is unirational but not rational. Equally, the Artin–Mumford construction of a
conic bundle over $\mathbb{P}^{2}$ gives an explicit unirational non-rational threefold.

The obstruction in the Artin–Mumford example is **2-torsion in $H^{3}(X, \mathbb{Z})$**: rationality of $X$ would force
this 2-torsion to be a birational invariant detected by the Brauer–Severi geometry, and an explicit calculation shows
the Artin–Mumford construction has non-trivial 2-torsion. (For cubic threefolds the obstruction is the *intermediate
Jacobian* — Clemens–Griffiths showed the Prym variety associated to the discriminant curve detects non-rationality.)

J. P. Murre extended some of these examples to characteristic $p > 0$, giving **strongly unirational non-rational**
threefolds in positive characteristic, by adapting the Clemens–Griffiths intermediate-Jacobian argument to the
positive-characteristic setting via crystalline cohomology.

(References: M. Artin–D. Mumford, "Some elementary examples of unirational varieties which are not rational," Proc.
London Math. Soc. 25 (1972), 75–95, appendix [`artin-mumford-cubic`](appendix-black-boxes.md#artin-mumford-cubic);
Deligne, Séminaire Bourbaki no. 402, 1971–72 (Lect. Notes 317); J. P. Murre, "Reduction of the proof of the
non-rationality of a non-singular cubic threefold to a result of Mumford," Compositio Math. 27 (1973), 63–82, appendix
[`murre-cubic-threefold`](appendix-black-boxes.md#murre-cubic-threefold).)

> **Slogan.** *In dim $\geq 3$, unirational does not imply rational.* The obstruction is finer than $\pi_{1}$: for the
> Artin–Mumford example, it is $H^{3}$-torsion; for the cubic threefold, it is the intermediate Jacobian.

### XI.1.4.4 — Rationally connected varieties (the modern definition)

<!-- source: XI.1.4 -->

The 2003 addendum by M. Raynaud to §XI.1.4 introduces the modern hierarchy. The key new vocabulary is **rationally
connected**.

**Definition (rationally connected — RC).** A normal integral projective $k$-variety $V$ is **rationally connected** if
there exist an integral finite-type $k$-scheme $T$ and a $k$-morphism

```text
F : T × ℙ¹ → V
```

such that the induced morphism

```text
T × ℙ¹ × ℙ¹ → V × V, \qquad (t, u, u′) ↦ (F(t, u), F(t, u′))
```

is **dominant**. (Equivalently, any two general points of $V$ are joined by a rational curve in $V$.)

**Definition (separably rationally connected — sRC).** If $char k = p > 0$, $V$ is **separably rationally connected** if
additionally the above two-point morphism is **generically smooth**. In characteristic zero, every dominant morphism is
generically smooth, so RC = sRC.

#### Geometric reformulations.

The definition admits several useful reformulations:

- *(two-point form)* For two general points $v_{1}, v_{2} \in V(\bar{k})$, there exists a rational curve
  $\mathbb{P}^{1} \to V$ whose image contains both $v_{1}$ and $v_{2}$.
- *(very-free curves)* For a general point $v \in V$, there exists a morphism $\mathbb{P}^{1} \to V$ passing through $v$
  whose normal bundle $N_{\mathbb{P}^{1}/V}$ is generated by global sections of strictly positive degree (a "very free"
  rational curve).
- *(chain form, equivalent for proper varieties)* Any two points of $V(\bar{k})$ can be joined by a *chain* of rational
  curves of bounded length.

The chain reformulation looks more flexible than the single-curve form, but for proper $V$ they are equivalent — if two
points are connected by a chain, then by a moduli-of-stable-maps argument they are connected by a single curve.

#### Hierarchy.

The chain of implications in characteristic zero (where everything is automatically separable):

```text
rational  ⊊  stably rational  ⊊  retract rational  ⊊  unirational  ⊊  rationally connected  ⊊  uniruled.
```

In characteristic $p > 0$ the picture is similar but with `sRC` substituted for `RC` in places where separability
matters:

```text
rational  ⊆  stably rational  ⊆  retract rational  ⊆  strongly unirational  ⊆  sRC  ⊆  RC  ⊆  uniruled,
```

with the inclusions sRC ⊆ RC and strongly unirational ⊆ weakly unirational both potentially proper.

(Reference for the hierarchy: O. Debarre, *Higher-dimensional algebraic geometry*, Springer 2001, Ch. 4; or Debarre's
Bourbaki seminar [`debarre-bourbaki-906`](appendix-black-boxes.md#debarre-bourbaki-906).)

### XI.1.4.5 — Kollár's theorem: sRC ⇒ trivial étale $\pi_{1}$

<!-- source: XI.1.4 -->

The headline result of the modern theory:

**Theorem (Kollár 1996, building on Kollár–Miyaoka–Mori 1992).** Let $V$ be a smooth projective separably rationally
connected variety over an algebraically closed field $k$. Then $\pi_{1}(V) = 0$.

*This result says:* the natural generalisation of XI.1.1, XI.1.2, and Serre's char-`0` transcendental theorem XI.1.4.1
to the much larger class of separably rationally connected varieties. In characteristic zero this includes all smooth
projective unirational and all smooth projective rationally connected varieties (since RC = sRC there); in
characteristic $p > 0$, only the sRC ones (strongly unirational, but also more — the class is strictly larger).

#### Idea of proof.

The proof is **purely algebraic**, in contrast to Serre's transcendental argument for the unirational case in
characteristic zero. The strategy:

1. **Very-free curves are abundant.** By the definition of sRC, through any general point $v \in V$ there passes a very
   free rational curve $f : \mathbb{P}^{1} \to V$ (with normal bundle generated by sections of positive degree).
1. **Étale covers lift very-free curves.** Let $g : V' \to V$ be a connected finite étale cover. Pull back $f$ to
   $f' : \mathbb{P}^{1} \to? V'$. Since $\mathbb{P}^{1}$ is simply connected (XI.1.1), the étale cover
   $g^{-1}(f(\mathbb{P}^{1}))$ of $\mathbb{P}^{1}$ splits — every connected component is isomorphic to $\mathbb{P}^{1}$.
1. **Connectivity.** Two general points of $V$ lift to two general points of $V'$ (cover by very free curves and use
   generic smoothness of the universal evaluation morphism, which is where the *separable* hypothesis enters). Hence
   $V'$ is connected.
1. **Degree conclusion.** Connectivity + the count of preimages of a general point forces $\deg g = 1$, so $V' \to V$ is
   an isomorphism. Hence $\pi_{1}(V) = 0$.

The role of the *separable* hypothesis is exactly at step 3: in inseparable rational connectedness, the universal
evaluation morphism is not generically smooth, and the lift of two points may collide before reaching the cover. The
non-separable rationally connected examples (Shioda-like) do *not* satisfy Kollár's hypothesis, and as we saw can have
non-trivial $\pi_{1}$.

(References: J. Kollár, *Rational Curves on Algebraic Varieties*, Ergebnisse 32, Springer 1996, §IV.3, appendix
[`kollar-rational-connected`](appendix-black-boxes.md#kollar-rational-connected); J. Kollár–Y. Miyaoka–S. Mori,
"Rationally connected varieties," J. Algebraic Geom. 1 (1992), 429–448, the foundational paper.)

> **Slogan.** *Separably rationally connected ⇒ simply connected.* The class is far larger than unirational; in char 0
> it includes all RC varieties. In char $p$, separability is load-bearing — exactly the same lesson the Zariski
> counterexample (file 54) will teach for X.1.3.

### XI.1.4.6 — Graber–Harris–Starr: RC families admit sections over curves

<!-- source: XI.1.4 -->

The companion to Kollár's theorem on RC fundamental groups:

**Theorem (Graber–Harris–Starr 2003).** Let $f : X \to B$ be a proper morphism of smooth complex varieties with $B$ a
curve. If the geometric generic fibre $\bar{X}_{\eta}$ is rationally connected, then $f$ admits a section.

*This result says:* rationally connectedness behaves well in *families* over curves — there is no "RC-monodromy"
obstruction to sections. This was the missing piece in the rationally-connected story when Kollár–Miyaoka–Mori first
proposed the definition in 1992.

#### Why this matters.

The Graber–Harris–Starr theorem confirms that **rational connectedness is the right family-level analogue of "simply
connected"**. Compare with the topological situation: if $f : X \to B$ is a continuous map of topological spaces and the
geometric fibres are contractible, then $f$ admits a section after possibly homotoping. The Graber–Harris–Starr theorem
is the algebraic-geometric counterpart in dimension 1 of the base.

A. J. de Jong and J. Starr proved the analogous result with $B$ allowed to be a higher-dimensional base, refining the
theorem to a complete theory of RC fibrations.

(References: T. Graber, J. Harris, J. Starr, "Families of rationally connected varieties," J. AMS 16 (2003), 57–67,
appendix [`graber-harris-starr-rational`](appendix-black-boxes.md#graber-harris-starr-rational); A. J. de Jong–J. Starr,
"Higher-dimensional analogues of the Graber–Harris–Starr theorem,"
[`de-jong-starr-rc-curve-base`](appendix-black-boxes.md#de-jong-starr-rc-curve-base).)

> **Slogan.** *RC is the family-rigid notion of "small $\pi_{1}$".* RC families over curves have sections; sRC fibres
> have trivial $\pi_{1}$.

### Closing of §XI.1.4 — what the SGA's "Remark XI.1.4" actually contains, after the 2003 update

The original 1960 remark in the SGA source contained only the Serre transcendental theorem. M. Raynaud's 2003 addendum
added the weak-vs-strong unirational discussion, the Shioda and Artin–Mumford counterexamples, and the modern RC
definition with Kollár's theorem. With the present gentle pass:

- §XI.1.4.1 covers Serre's theorem (original 1960 remark).
- §§XI.1.4.2–XI.1.4.3 cover the Shioda + Artin–Mumford + Murre examples (Raynaud 2003 addendum).
- §§XI.1.4.4–XI.1.4.6 cover the modern Kollár + Graber–Harris–Starr story (Raynaud 2003 addendum and beyond).

The gentle treatment is **deliberate** — these are the modern results that contextualise where Exposé XI sits in the
larger story of $\pi_{1}$ of proper varieties. The lesson:

> *Smooth projective varieties with "enough rational curves" have small $\pi_{1}$.* The right notion of "enough rational
> curves" — separably rationally connected — gives **trivial** $\pi_{1}$ in any characteristic.

We computed $\pi_{1}$ of projective spaces (XI.1.1, two proofs), of rational normal proper varieties (XI.1.2 with
Grassmann and $G/P$ applications), and of unirational normal varieties (XI.1.4, finiteness). The gentle subsections

Next, file 53 turns to **abelian varieties** — the opposite extreme to projective space. Where $\mathbb{P}^{r}$ has
trivial $\pi_{1}$, abelian varieties have the *richest* $\pi_{1}$ among proper smooth varieties of a given dimension:
the Tate module $T\cdot(A) = \lim_{n} {}_{nA}(k)$ of rank `2g` away from $p$ and of rank $\nu \leq g$ at $p$. The "true"
fundamental group of $A$, incorporating the infinitesimal flat kernels, is rank `2g` at every prime (the **Nori
fundamental group scheme**) — the abelian case is exactly where the difference between étale $\pi_{1}$ and flat
$\pi_{1}$ first becomes visible.
