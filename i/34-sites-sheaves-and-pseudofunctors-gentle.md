# Sites, sheaves, and pseudofunctors

## What this file is about

Background for a more abstract chapter. The pictures so far have all relied on the word "cover": a smaller piece sitting
over a larger one, with a rule for gluing several smaller pieces back into the larger. In ordinary topology the rule is
given by open sets and unions. Grothendieck's idea was to keep the rule and drop the open sets.

What remains is a category equipped with a vocabulary for which collections of arrows should count as covers, and an
axiom saying that data spread across a cover and agreeing on overlaps comes from a single piece of data on the whole.
This file collects that vocabulary. It is the language in which the next chapter will state a single grand theorem: many
of the structures that arise on geometric objects can be reassembled from their behaviour on any sufficiently fine
cover.

## Module A: Sites and Grothendieck topologies

The starting observation. A topology on a topological space $X$ is determined by which collections of open subsets cover
which opens. The points of $X$ and the open sets themselves are not required: the data of "open subset" can be read off
from "open inclusion morphism", and the data of "covering" is the information ${U_{i} \subset U}$ such that
$\bigcup_{i} U_{i} = U$. Once we have phrased the data this way, we can imitate it in any category: a **Grothendieck
topology** assigns, to each object, a class of collections of arrows ("covering families"), subject to the right axioms.

### Sieves

A first technical convenience. Fix a category $\mathcal{E}$ and an object $S \in Ob \mathcal{E}$. A **sieve** on $S$ is
a sub-presheaf of the representable presheaf `Hom_ℰ(–, S)`. Concretely: a class $R$ of arrows into $S$, closed under
precomposition (if $f : T \to S$ is in $R$ and $g : T' \to T$ is any arrow, then $f \circ g$ is in $R$). The sieve
**generated** by a single arrow $f : T \to S$ is

```text
(f)  =  { f ∘ g : T′ → S  :  g any arrow with target T }.
```

The sieve generated by a family ${f_{i} : T_{i} \to S}_{i}$ is the union of the $(f_{i})$.

Sieves are the formal way to talk about "the open subsets covered by a collection". A pretopology talks about the
collections; the generated sieve loses no information.

### Grothendieck pretopologies and sites

A **Grothendieck pretopology** on a category $\mathcal{E}$ (assumed to have fibre products) is, for each
$S \in Ob \mathcal{E}$, a class of **covering families** ${U_{i} \to S}_{i}$ of arrows into $S$, subject to:

1. **Isomorphism.** If $V \to S$ is an isomorphism, the singleton ${V \to S}$ is a covering.
1. **Stability under base change.** If ${U_{i} \to S}_{i}$ is a covering and $T \to S$ is any arrow, then the pullback
   family ${U_{i} \times_{S} T \to T}_{i}$ is a covering of $T$.
1. **Transitivity.** If ${U_{i} \to S}_{i}$ is a covering and ${V_{ij} \to U_{i}}_{j}$ is a covering for each $i$, then
   the composite family ${V_{ij} \to U_{i} \to S}_{i, j}$ is a covering of $S$.

A **site** is a category equipped with a Grothendieck topology (= the topology of sieves generated by a pretopology).
The distinction between pretopology and topology is technical: two pretopologies generate the same topology iff they
have the same sheaves. We will speak of pretopologies because they are easier to write down; the only place we use
sieves directly is when quoting the formal sheaf condition.

### Standard sites on a scheme

Five sites recur throughout algebraic geometry. They are nested by inclusion of covering families:

```text
Zariski  ⊂  Nisnevich  ⊂  étale  ⊂  fppf  ⊂  fpqc.
```

Fix a base scheme $S$ and a category $(Sch / S)$ of $S$-schemes (or its small variant). On the same category, the five
sites agree on objects and morphisms, but differ on what counts as a covering family ${U_{i} \to U}_{i}$:

- **Zariski.** The $U_{i} \to U$ are open immersions and are jointly surjective.
- **Nisnevich.** The $U_{i} \to U$ are étale and jointly surjective, and additionally for every point $u \in U$ some
  $U_{i}$ admits a point $u_{i}$ with the same residue field.
- **Étale.** The $U_{i} \to U$ are étale (file 04, file 05) and jointly surjective.
- **Fppf** (= _fidèlement plat et de présentation finie_). The $U_{i} \to U$ are flat (file 04, file 24), locally of
  finite presentation, and jointly surjective.
- **Fpqc** (= _fidèlement plat et quasi-compact_). The $U_{i} \to U$ are jointly surjective and the resulting
  $\coprod_{i} U_{i} \to U$ is faithfully flat (file 24) and quasi-compact in the appropriate refined sense (cf. Vistoli
  §2.3.2 for the precise definition; the naive "faithfully flat" is not closed under base change).

We will use the Zariski and étale sites by name throughout files 35–38. The fpqc site is named in file 38's closing
pointer as the ambient site for Exposé VIII's faithfully flat descent statements; its precise definition is left to
Vistoli.

### Sheaves on a site

Fix a site $(\mathcal{E}, \tau)$ (we drop $\tau$ when the topology is clear). A **presheaf of sets** on $\mathcal{E}$ is
a functor $F : \mathcal{E}^{\circ} \to Set$. A **sheaf** on $(\mathcal{E}, \tau)$ is a presheaf $F$ such that, for every
covering family ${U_{i} \to S}_{i}$ of every $S \in Ob \mathcal{E}$, the **equaliser sequence**

```text
F(S)  ⟶  ∏_i F(U_i)  ⇉  ∏_{i, j} F(U_i ×_S U_j)
```

is exact: the left arrow is an equaliser of the two right arrows (restriction along the two projections
$U_{i} \times_{S} U_{j} \to U_{i}$ and $U_{i} \times_{S} U_{j} \to U_{j}$).

Concretely: a section over $S$ is the same as a compatible family of sections over the $U_{i}$, where "compatible" means
"agreeing on every overlap $U_{i} \times_{S} U_{j}$". This generalises the classical sheaf condition for open covers of
a topological space (file 03).

We write $Sh(\mathcal{E}, \tau)$ for the category of sheaves of sets on $(\mathcal{E}, \tau)$ (with morphisms = natural
transformations of presheaves) and $PSh(\mathcal{E})$ for presheaves. Variants $Sh(\mathcal{E}, Ab)$,
$Sh(\mathcal{E}, Grp)$, ... of sheaves of abelian groups, of groups, etc., are obtained by replacing `Set` with the
relevant category.

### Examples

Specialise to schemes:

- A presheaf on the small Zariski site of $S$ is a presheaf in the classical sense (file 03). The structure sheaf
  $\mathcal{O}_{S}$ is a sheaf of rings.
- A representable presheaf `h_X = Hom_S(–, X)` for $X / S$ is always a sheaf in the **Zariski**, **étale**, **fppf**,
  and **fpqc** topologies. ("Every scheme is a sheaf in the fpqc topology" is a baby version of descent: the categorical
  sheaf condition holds for the data `Hom(–, X)`.)
- A quasi-coherent $\mathcal{O}_{S}$-module gives a sheaf on the small Zariski site of $S$. In the étale and fpqc
  topologies, quasi-coherent sheaves give _fibred categories_, not presheaves of sets — the values are themselves
  categories ($QCoh(U)$), and that is why we need pseudofunctors.

### What a sheaf-of-categories is, briefly

When $F$ is a presheaf of _categories_ (so $F(S) \in Cat$ for each $S$), the sheaf condition becomes a _limit_ condition
in `Cat`: $F(S)$ is the limit of the diagram

```text
∏_i F(U_i)  ⇉  ∏_{i, j} F(U_i ×_S U_j)  ⇛  ∏_{i, j, k} F(U_i ×_S U_j ×_S U_k)
```

(now with three parallel arrows on the right and a triple-overlap correction term). This is what gets called a **stack**
in modern language. SGA VI does not use this language directly, but the descent isomorphism of file 38 §VI.12 is its
categorical backbone: a global functor on a fibred category is determined by fibre-wise data plus gluing instructions.

## Module B: 2-categories and the 2-category `Cat`

The starting observation. The category of _categories_ is not just a category. Between two categories $\mathcal{C}$ and
$\mathcal{D}$ there is a category $Fun(\mathcal{C}, \mathcal{D})$ whose objects are functors and whose morphisms are
natural transformations. So `Cat` has objects (categories), 1-cells (functors), and 2-cells (natural transformations) —
three layers of structure. The package is a **2-category**.

### 2-categories

A **2-category** $\mathcal{K}$ consists of:

- a class of **0-cells** (also called objects) $A, B, C, \cdots$;
- for each pair $(A, B)$, a category $\mathcal{K}(A, B)$ whose objects are **1-cells** $f : A \to B$ and whose morphisms
  are **2-cells** $\alpha : f \Longrightarrow g$;
- for each triple $(A, B, C)$, a horizontal composition functor
  $\mathcal{K}(B, C) \times \mathcal{K}(A, B) \to \mathcal{K}(A, C)$;
- identity 1-cells $id_{A} : A \to A$ for each $A$.

Subject to: associativity and unit laws for horizontal composition (which require natural isomorphisms in the strict
sense — see "lax / pseudo / strict 2-category" below), and the **interchange law** between horizontal and vertical
composition of 2-cells.

The minimal abstract definition; we use it only to name a target. No 2-cell of a 2-cell ever appears in this project.

### `Cat` as a 2-category

`Cat` (let us be honest about size: small or $\mathcal{U}$-small categories for some Grothendieck universe
$\mathcal{U}$) is a 2-category with:

- 0-cells = (small) categories;
- 1-cells = functors;
- 2-cells = natural transformations.

The horizontal composition of 2-cells is the **whiskering** / horizontal composition of natural transformations
($(\alpha * \beta)_{X} = \alpha_{F'X} \circ G(\beta_{X})$ for $\alpha : F \Longrightarrow F'$ and
$\beta : G \Longrightarrow G'$, with $F, F' : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ and $G, G' : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{E}$).
The interchange law holds; identity 1-cells are identity functors; identity 2-cells are identity natural
transformations.

The relevant fact for SGA VI: a "functor $\mathcal{E} \to Cat$" weakly compatible with composition (= a _pseudofunctor_)
is _not_ a 1-cell of `Cat`-as-a-2-category; it is extra data, equipping the naive functor with coherence 2-cells.

### 2-functors, pseudofunctors, lax functors

Let $\mathcal{K}, \mathcal{L}$ be 2-categories. A 2-functor $F : \mathcal{K} \to \mathcal{L}$ strictly preserves
composition: $F(g) \circ F(f) = F(g \circ f)$ on the nose. A **pseudofunctor** weakens this: there is, for each
composable pair $(f, g)$, an _invertible_ 2-cell

```text
ϕ_{f, g}  :  F(g) ∘ F(f)  ⟹  F(g ∘ f),
```

natural in $(f, g)$, satisfying coherence axioms (the pentagon identity, see Module C). A **lax functor** drops
invertibility, allowing a _non-invertible_ 2-cell. We meet only pseudofunctors; SGA VI's framework is pseudofunctors,
never lax.

Synonyms and warnings. _Pseudofunctor_ and _normalised lax 2-functor_ with invertible coherence are the same thing.
Bénabou's original term was **morphism of bicategories**. Different communities draw the line in slightly different
places; the data is the same.

### Adjoints and equivalences in a 2-category

In `Cat`, an adjoint pair $(F \dashv G)$ consists of functors $F : \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D}$ and
$G : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$ with natural transformations $\eta : id_{\mathcal{C}} \Longrightarrow G F$ (unit) and
$\epsilon : F G \Longrightarrow id_{\mathcal{D}}$ (counit) satisfying the triangle identities. In any 2-category
$\mathcal{K}$, the same definition makes sense for two 1-cells $f : A \to B$ and $g : B \to A$ and two 2-cells
$\eta : id_{A} \Longrightarrow g \circ f$, $\epsilon : f \circ g \Longrightarrow id_{B}$ satisfying the same triangles.
The motivating example: any geometric morphism between toposes gives an adjoint pair of functors, and the geometric
morphisms themselves form 2-cells in the 2-category of toposes.

An **equivalence** in a 2-category is an adjoint pair where $\eta, \epsilon$ are 2-isomorphisms (invertible 2-cells). In
`Cat` this specialises to the usual notion of "adjoint equivalence", and is equivalent to "fully faithful + essentially
surjective" up to choice (for that reason we keep using the latter as a working criterion).

Why we have brought this up. In file 37 §VI.10, a bi-fibred category $\mathcal{F} \to \mathcal{E}$ provides, for each
$f : T \to S$, an adjoint pair $(f^{*} \dashv f_{*})$ between $\mathcal{F}_{S}$ and $\mathcal{F}_{T}$ — the inverse and
direct images. This is one of the recurring patterns of fibred-category theory.

## Module C: Pseudofunctors $\mathcal{E}^{\circ} \to Cat$ and the coherence pentagon

We arrive at the central definition. Throughout, $\mathcal{E}$ is an ordinary 1-category (no 2-cells of its own),
$\mathcal{E}^{\circ}$ its opposite, and `Cat` is the 2-category just described.

### Definition

A **pseudofunctor** $\varphi : \mathcal{E}^{\circ} \to Cat$ consists of the following data, which we _list_ before the
axioms:

1. For each $S \in Ob \mathcal{E}$, a category $\varphi(S)$.

1. For each arrow $f : T \to S$ in $\mathcal{E}$, a functor $f^{*} := \varphi(f) : \varphi(S) \to \varphi(T)$.
   (Contravariant — the arrow reverses direction because we are working with $\mathcal{E}^{\circ}$.)

1. For each composable pair $(f, g)$ with $T --f--> S --g--> R$, a _natural isomorphism_ of functors

    ```text
    c_{f, g}  :  f^* ∘ g^*  ⟹  (g ∘ f)^*    :   ϕ(R) → ϕ(T).
    ```

    (Contravariance flips the order of composition.)

1. For each $S \in Ob \mathcal{E}$, a _natural isomorphism_

    ```text
    ε_S  :  (id_S)^*  ⟹  id_{ϕ(S)}  :   ϕ(S) → ϕ(S).
    ```

These data must satisfy:

- **Pentagon coherence.** For each composable triple $(f, g, h)$ with $T --f--> S --g--> R --h--> Q$ and each
  $\xi \in \varphi(Q)$, the two natural isomorphisms

    ```text
    f^* g^* h^*(ξ)  ⟹  (h g f)^*(ξ)
    ```

    obtained by pasting $c_{g, h}$ then $c_{f, hg}$, and by pasting $c_{f, g}$ then $c_{gf, h}$, agree:

    ```text
    c_{f, hg} ∘ (f^* * c_{g, h})    =    c_{gf, h} ∘ (c_{f, g} * h^*).
    ```

    Both sides are natural transformations $f^{*} g^{*} h^{*} \Longrightarrow (hgf)^{*}$; the pentagon says they are equal.

- **Normalisation.** For every $f : T \to S$, the composites
  $c_{id_{T}, f} \circ (\epsilon_{T} * f^{*}) : f^{*} \Longrightarrow f^{*}$ and
  $c_{f, id_{S}} \circ (f^{*} * \epsilon_{S}) : f^{*} \Longrightarrow f^{*}$ are both equal to the identity 2-cell on
  $f^{*}$.

A **strict** (or **normalised**) pseudofunctor is one where $(id_{S})^{*} = id_{\varphi(S)}$ and $\epsilon_{S}$ is the
identity. We will work with normalised pseudofunctors throughout; every pseudofunctor is isomorphic to a normalised one,
in the precise 2-categorical sense.

#### What the pentagon is doing

The pentagon is the _associativity coherence_ for the contravariant assignment $f \mapsto f^{*}$. We have three
composable arrows $T \to S \to R \to Q$. There are two ways to compose the "three pull-backs" into a single one of
$(hgf)^{*}$:

- first contract `g, h` to `(hg)`, then `f, hg` to `(hgf)`;
- first contract `f, g` to `(gf)`, then `gf, h` to `(hgf)`.

The pentagon says these two contractions agree (after identifying both sides with $(hgf)^{*}$ via two pastings of
$c$'s). It is the 2-cell version of the equation $(hg) \circ f = h \circ (gf)$ for arrows in a 1-category — that
equation holds on the nose for arrows; for the 2-cells `c_{–, –}` it holds only after one stipulates the pentagon
explicitly.

### Mac Lane's coherence theorem

A pseudofunctor's data includes one coherence 2-cell $c_{f, g}$ per composable pair. Composable triples give the
pentagon; composable quadruples raise the question of whether all _higher_ coherences (e.g. for four composable arrows
there are five ways to contract) follow automatically.

**Mac Lane's coherence theorem** says they do.

> _This result says: in a pseudofunctor (or, more generally, in any monoidal-category-like structure), the pentagon and
> normalisation force every diagram of $c$'s to commute. Every two parallel pasting compositions of coherence
> isomorphisms agree._

We cite this as the black box [`mac-lane-coherence`](appendix-black-boxes.md#mac-lane-coherence). The original is Mac
Lane's _Natural associativity and commutativity_ (Rice University Studies 49, 1963); a textbook treatment is Mac Lane
_Categories for the Working Mathematician_, Chapter VII §2. Modern 2-categorical refinements (e.g. for tricategories)
add further coherences, but at the level of pseudofunctors $\mathcal{E}^{\circ} \to Cat$ the pentagon plus normalisation
is enough.

### Examples

Three examples of pseudofunctors $\mathcal{E}^{\circ} \to Cat$, in increasing order of subtlety.

**Example 1** (constant pseudofunctor). Fix any category $\mathcal{D}$. Define
$\varphi_{\mathcal{D}} : \mathcal{E}^{\circ} \to Cat$ by $\varphi_{\mathcal{D}}(S) = \mathcal{D}$ for every $S$,
$\varphi_{\mathcal{D}}(f) = id_{\mathcal{D}}$ for every $f$, and $c_{f, g} = id$ for every composable pair. This is a
strict pseudofunctor (in fact a 2-functor sending everything to a single 0-cell).

**Example 2** (presheaf-of-slices). Take $\mathcal{E} = (Sch / S)$ for a fixed base scheme $S$. Define
$\varphi_{slice}(T) = (Sch / T)$ — the slice category of$S$-schemes equipped with a chosen morphism to$T$.
For$f : T \to S$, the pull-back
functor`ϕ_{slice}(f) : (Sch / S) → (Sch / T)`is$(X \to S) \mapsto (X \times_{S} T \to T)$. The coherence
isomorphism$c_{f, g}$ is the canonical isomorphism

```text
(X ×_R S) ×_S T  ≅  X ×_R T
```

— the "associativity of fibre product". The pentagon for $c_{f, g}$ becomes the standard four-fold associativity diagram
for fibre products of schemes, which holds _up to canonical isomorphism, not on the nose_. This is _the_ example whose
strictification fails: there is no way to choose actual fibre products to make $(X \times_{R} S) \times_{S} T$ literally
equal to $X \times_{R} T$ across all $(X, R, S, T)$. The non-strictness of $c_{f, g}$ is the obstruction.

**Example 3** (the QCoh pseudofunctor). For $\mathcal{E} = (Sch / S)$, define `QCoh : (Sch / S)° → Cat` by $QCoh(T) =$
the category of quasi-coherent $\mathcal{O}_{T}$-modules. For $f : T \to R$, the pull-back functor
$f^{*} : QCoh(R) \to QCoh(T)$ is the standard one (file 03 gives the affine-local definition; full account in Vistoli
§3.2). The coherence isomorphism $c_{f, g}$ is the canonical iso $f^{*} g^{*} M \cong (g \circ f)^{*} M$ of
quasi-coherent sheaves, coming from associativity of $\otimes$. The pentagon: associativity of $\otimes$ is strict, but
its lifts to $f^{*}$ involve canonical-isomorphism pasting, and the four-pull-back diagram commutes only up to the
canonical iso, not on the nose. Pentagon = the four-pull-back diagram.

The QCoh pseudofunctor is the example whose _strictification question_ motivates fibred categories. Naive pull-back of
quasi-coherent sheaves is not associative on the nose, but the fibred-category formalism handles the failure of strict
associativity cleanly. Exposé VIII's faithfully flat descent statement for quasi-coherent sheaves is the geometric
payoff: _for an fpqc morphism $f : T \to S$, every "compatible family of quasi-coherent sheaves on a covering" descends
to a quasi-coherent sheaf on $S$_. We name this for context and defer the precise formulation to a future Exposé VIII
pass (cf. file 38's closing pointer).
